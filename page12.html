01 / THE CONTEXT

A complex business.
A fragmented view.

The portal served affiliates and introducing brokers who refer traders and earn commissions linked to trading activity. Some also managed sub-partners, creating a network with several layers of relationships.

As the legacy platform grew, its reporting became fragmented. Partners had to move between reports to piece together their earnings, clients, and performance.

THE DESIGN CHALLENGE

Make the business understandable before asking partners to act.

The redesign needed to connect information across the partner journey, while working within existing commission rules and operational constraints.

Where I contributed

I joined at the start of the six-week inception phase, leading research planning, user interviews, journey mapping, and stakeholder workshops. I translated that discovery into a future-state journey and initial flows that informed roadmap, prioritisation, and technical planning.

02 / DISCOVERY

Learn the business.
Then follow the partner.

I started with domain experts and the product itself, then used interviews to understand the work happening around the portal.

01 / UNDERSTAND

Build a shared language

I worked with domain experts to understand the referral model, commission logic, and partner motivations. A test account and product walkthrough helped me trace onboarding, reporting, and everyday tasks before writing research questions.

Why it mattered A reporting problem could come from the interface, the business rules, or the process behind it. Discovery needed to distinguish all three.

02 / PLAN

Research beyond the screen

I planned semi-structured interviews around partners’ recurring tasks, decisions, and challenges. Recruitment considered experience level, geographic market, and the size of a partner’s referral network.

The discussion guide moved from role and responsibilities to top tasks, a portal deep dive, and open feedback. It was reviewed with the Product Owner and team.

Learn from adjacent experiences

03 / DEFINE THE PROBLEM

Connect the research
to a design response.

I expanded the existing journey map with interview findings, organising pain points around partner goals. Four problem areas became the focus of the revised brief.

WHAT WAS GETTING IN THE WAYDESIGN DIRECTION
01

Performance was scattered

Rebates, clients, deposits, and performance required separate reports. Tier and earning-level visibility were limited.

Bring the overview together

Surface key metrics in one dashboard, with routes into the detail behind them.

02

Acquisition lacked context

Partners had little visibility into which marketing channels or campaigns contributed to acquisition and value.

Make performance interpretable

Connect channel information with the reporting partners use to assess their business.

03

Data did not explain the next step

Complex reporting made it hard to connect current performance with progress and potential earnings.

Show progress with context

Pair trends and rebate tiers with clearer explanations and planning tools.

04

Rebates depended on manual checks

Weekly operational approvals created a delay before rebates appeared and became available for withdrawal.

Separate visibility from processing

Clarify the earning and approval journey. Faster processing also needs operational and technical changes.

THE REFRAMED BRIEF

How might we help partners understand their business and make informed decisions about what comes next?

FROM PROBLEMS TO PRIORITIES

Make the trade-offs visible

I facilitated a stakeholder workshop to examine root causes, separating technical constraints, business decisions, and interaction problems.

We clustered ideas and used dot voting to prioritise directions. The resulting future-state journey connected the opportunities into a clearer end-to-end flow.

A boundary worth keeping A clearer interface can explain rebate processing; it cannot remove a manual approval step on its own.

02 / FLOWS BEFORE POLISH

Resolve the connections between screens.

I developed initial flows and reviewed them with stakeholders from technical and business perspectives. Feedback helped refine the designs presented at the end of inception.

Documenting those decisions gave the delivery team a starting point for more detailed flow design during sprints.

03 / A FLEXIBLE OVERVIEW

Give reporting a useful hierarchy.

The dashboard direction included customisable widgets, earnings trends, rebate-level visibility, and marketing performance information.

A proposed rebate calculator connected planning inputs with potential earnings. These tools were intended to make reporting more useful for partners with different goals and network sizes.

DESIGNING FOR DELIVERY

A shared foundation
for the next phase.

Work with the implementation context

The component library used Material Design 2 as its foundation to align with the project’s Flutter requirement. The visual language was customised to the client’s brand guidelines.

I worked with the visual designer to identify component gaps and define consistent navigation, layout, and interaction patterns. Storybook documented component usage, states, behaviour, and accessibility criteria.

MY CONTRIBUTION

05 / OUTCOMES & REFLECTION

Clarity was the deliverable.
Confidence was the goal.

What the work established

The inception phase produced a research-informed direction, a future-state journey, and initial designs that informed product prioritisation and technical planning.

Qualitative feedback highlighted clearer information, more intuitive workflows, and greater confidence in performance tracking. These findings describe how participants perceived the design; they do not quantify a change in live performance.

What I would measure next

In follow-up validation, I would test whether partners can locate and explain a rebate, understand its approval state, and find the relevant client or network report without assistance.

Task completion, interpretation errors, and time spent switching between reports would help assess whether the design actually improves understanding.

WHAT I TOOK FORWARD

Making a complex product simpler starts with understanding what cannot be simplified away.

Commission logic, network relationships, and approval processes still need to exist. The design task is to make those relationships legible, while being clear about which changes require more than a new interface.
